Summary

After preparing the data, the next step will be modelling. Modeling helps us understand how variables relate to each other. Models allow us to predict outcomes for new observations. Any prediction function demonstrates this - it takes new patient data and estimates their probability of the outcome occurring. This is crucial for clinical decision-making.

In this practical, we follow the recommendations of Efthimiou et al., The BMJ (2024). Their guide emphasizes that a clinical prediction model should be assessed using both discrimination—how well it separates patients with and without the outcome—and calibration—how closely predicted risks agree with observed outcomes. It also stresses that performance measured on the development data can be optimistic, so internal validation should be used to obtain a more honest estimate before considering model selection or clinical usefulness.

Keywords summary

Field Definition
Dxy Somers’ D rank correlation; a discrimination measure related to AUC by AUC = (Dxy + 1) / 2.
R² A model-specific pseudo-R² measure; it is not the percentage of outcome variance explained.
Intercept Calibration intercept; values near 0 indicate little overall over- or underprediction.
Slope Calibration slope; values near 1 indicate appropriate spread of predictions.
Emax Maximum absolute calibration error.
D Discrimination index derived from the model likelihood ratio, calculated as (χ²_model − 1) / n; larger values indicate more information than the null model.
U Unreliability index: the lack of fit remaining after overall calibration; values near 0 are preferred.
Q Overall quality index, calculated as Q = D − U; larger values are preferred.
B Brier score: the mean squared difference between observed outcomes and predicted probabilities; smaller values are preferred.
g Gini mean difference of the linear predictor (Xβ), measuring separation on the log-odds scale.
gp Gini mean difference on the predicted-probability scale; in rms, this is not an adjustment for the number of predictors.

TipQuestions for discussion and answers

Question 1. How should the categorized coefficients and odds ratios be interpreted?

Answer. Each category is compared with the reference category, the bottom 20% of x1. The top 20% has approximately 20.6 times the odds of the event relative to that reference group; the other odds ratios describe their corresponding category contrasts.

Question 2. How does categorization change prediction and interpretation?

Answer. It makes results easier to communicate as group comparisons and can represent thresholds, but it discards information within each group and makes results depend on the chosen cut-points. A continuous or spline model should therefore also be considered.

TipQuestions for discussion and answers

Question 1. How should the spline model output be interpreted?

Answer. The spline represents the overall association between x1 and the log-odds as a smooth curve. Individual spline-basis coefficients are not interpretable as separate one-unit effects; predicted probabilities or a plot of the fitted curve are more informative.

Question 2. What does the odds ratio mean for a non-linear effect?

Answer. There is no single constant odds ratio for all values of x1. The odds ratio depends on the comparison points, so it should be reported for clinically meaningful contrasts or shown through predicted risks.

Question 3. Could the spline be overfit or underfit?

Answer. Three knots provide more flexibility than a linear term but can still overfit a small dataset. The apparent improvement should therefore be checked with bootstrap or cross-validation.

TipQuestions for discussion and answers

Question 1. How should the multivariable spline model be interpreted?

Answer. x1 and x2 are allowed to have curved effects, while the categorical predictors remain additive terms. The model therefore estimates a flexible joint relationship, not one constant linear effect for each continuous predictor.

Question 2. How should the odds ratios for the spline terms be interpreted?

Answer. The reported odds ratios are basis-term or reference-contrast summaries and should not be read as a single global odds ratio per one-unit increase. Use predicted probabilities or pairwise contrasts at selected values instead.

Question 3. Is Model 5 more flexible than Model 4?

Answer. Yes. It has spline terms for x1 and x2, so it can represent curvature, but the extra flexibility increases parameterization and overfitting risk. Its apparent gains must be weighed against AIC and validated performance.

Question. How should the AIC values be compared, and which model is preferred by AIC?

Answer. AIC compares relative fit after penalizing model complexity; lower values are preferred. Model 1 has the lowest AIC in this analysis. This does not mean it is automatically best for every purpose, because discrimination, calibration, validation, and clinical utility also matter.

B. AUROC (Area Under the ROC Curve)

AUC measures the model’s discriminative ability, or how well it ranks observations from the two outcome classes (e.g., 1 vs. 0). What constitutes useful discrimination depends on the clinical context and outcome prevalence.

  • AUC = 0.5 → no better than random
  • AUC = 1.0 → perfect discrimination
  • AUC > 0.8 is generally considered good

Use: Higher AUC means better ability to rank predicted probabilities correctly (e.g., a true positive is ranked higher than a false positive).

C. Calibration Plot

The calibration plot is used to detect if the model is producing well-calibrated probabilities, which is important for decision-making
Each plot compares:

  • Apparent line (dashed): fit on the same data used to train the model (i.e., not corrected for optimism).
  • Bias-corrected line (solid): fit after internal validation (bootstrapping with 200 repetitions), which adjusts for overfitting.
  • The closer both lines are to the 45-degree diagonal, the better the calibration of the model — i.e., predicted probabilities match actual outcomes.

D. R-Square

R² is a model-specific summary measure. For logistic regression, pseudo-R² values should not be interpreted as the proportion of variance explained. Based on the model:

  • R² = 0 → no explanatory power
  • R² = 1 → perfect explanation (rare)
  • Values are usually much lower than in linear regression — even 0.2–0.4 can be considered good for logistic models.

E. Additional discrimination, calibration, and prediction-accuracy indices

The additional rms indexes describe different aspects of model performance beyond a single measure such as AUC:

  • D summarizes discrimination on the model-information scale; larger values indicate more separation between outcome groups.
  • U measures unreliability related to calibration; values close to zero are preferred.
  • Q combines discrimination and calibration as Q = D − U; larger values are preferred.
  • B is the Brier score, measuring the accuracy of predicted probabilities; smaller values are preferred.
  • g measures separation on the linear-predictor (log-odds) scale, while gp measures separation on the predicted-probability scale; larger values indicate greater separation.

For Models 1–5, rms::validate() returns these indexes together with the following validation columns:

  • index.orig: the apparent performance of the original fitted model, evaluated on the full development dataset before optimism correction.
  • training: the average performance of each bootstrap-refitted model when it is evaluated on the bootstrap sample used to fit it.
  • test: the average performance of each bootstrap-refitted model when it is evaluated on the original development dataset, which acts as the comparison sample for estimating optimism.
  • optimism: the average difference between training and test; it estimates how much performance is inflated when a model is evaluated on the data used to fit it.
  • index.corrected: index.orig - optimism, giving an optimism-corrected estimate of internal performance.

The bootstrap procedure and the corresponding values for all five models are shown in the following Summary of Model Comparison section. There, we first examine apparent development-sample performance and then compare it with bootstrap optimism-corrected performance.

Summary of Model Comparison

We will use two complementary ways to assess model performance. Both use the same discrimination, calibration, and prediction-accuracy measures, but differ in whether the model is evaluated on its development data or after bootstrap optimism correction:

  1. Apparent, development-sample performance: each model is evaluated on the same observations used to fit it. This describes how well the model fits the development data, but it is usually optimistic because the model has already seen those observations.
  2. Bootstrap optimism-corrected performance: the modelling and assessment process is repeated in bootstrap samples, and the apparent optimism is estimated and subtracted from the original performance. This provides a more realistic estimate of expected internal performance in new patients from a similar population.

The tables below are generated from the fitted models and the bootstrap validation objects so that the reported values remain synchronized with the code.

The bootstrap validation procedure used for both tables is:

set.seed(2025)
models <- list(model1, model2, model3, model4, model5)
bootstrap_validation <- lapply(models, function(model) {
  validate(model, method = "boot", B = 200)
})

criteria_rows <- c("D", "U", "Q", "B", "g", "gp")

Apparent, development-sample performance

The first table reports the apparent performance of Models 1–5. The code extracts the index.orig column from each rms::validate() result. These are development-sample estimates: useful for showing how the calculations work, but not sufficient evidence that a model will perform equally well in new patients.

Bootstrap optimism-corrected performance

The second table reports the index.corrected values from the same bootstrap validation. This follows the BMJ guide’s principle that the complete modelling process should be repeated in the resampled data, including any model-selection steps if they were used. Here, each of the five fitted models is validated with 200 bootstrap repetitions. For AUC, the optimism-corrected Dxy is converted using AUC = (Dxy + 1) / 2. For calibration, an intercept closer to 0 and a slope closer to 1 are preferred. These corrected values are more relevant than apparent values when comparing expected performance in new patients.

corrected_rms <- do.call(rbind, lapply(bootstrap_validation, function(validation) {

The resulting table shows the optimism-corrected estimates for each model. Comparing it with the apparent table illustrates the effect of optimism correction: the apparent performance of the more flexible models decreases more noticeably, while the corrected calibration slopes below 1 suggest that some predictions are too extreme after accounting for overfitting.

Models 4 and 5 have the strongest apparent discrimination and pseudo-R², but bootstrap correction reduces their expected performance. The corrected calibration slopes below 1 for Models 2–5 indicate some overfitting or overly extreme predictions after internal validation; Models 4 and 5 should therefore not be described as clearly superior from apparent AUC alone. Model 1 has the lowest AIC, but AIC is a fit-and-complexity criterion rather than an optimism-corrected predictive-performance measure. The final choice should consider corrected discrimination, calibration, model complexity, and clinical usefulness.

Efthimiou et al. recommend assessing both discrimination and calibration, performing internal validation, and basing final model selection on optimism-corrected performance; when models perform similarly, a simpler model may be preferable. See Efthimiou et al., The BMJ (2024), BMJ 2024;386:e078276, doi: 10.1136/bmj-2023-078276.

  • Models 4 and 5 (the more flexible multivariable specifications) have the strongest apparent discrimination, but their performance decreases more after bootstrap optimism correction.
  • Model 1 has the lowest AIC and, in this analysis, the strongest optimism-corrected AUC, D, and Q, with a calibration slope closest to 1. Model 3 gives a similar corrected result.

For a related perspective on validating prediction models for patient outcomes and individualized treatment effects, see Chapter 19 of Comparative Effectiveness and Personalized Medicine Research Using Real-World Data.

Teaching conclusion. Model 4 remains a useful and interpretable teaching example because it includes all available predictors. However, its apparent performance should not be interpreted as evidence that it is the best model. Final selection should consider optimism-corrected performance, uncertainty, external validation, and clinical usefulness.

TipQuestions for discussion and answers

Question. Which model is best overall?

Answer. There is no single winner based on apparent metrics alone. Models 4 and 5 look strongest before correction, but Model 1 has the lowest AIC and the strongest optimism-corrected discrimination and Q in this analysis; Model 3 is similar. Model 4 remains a reasonable teaching example because it is interpretable and includes the available predictors, but it should not be declared the best model without considering uncertainty, external validation, and clinical usefulness.
